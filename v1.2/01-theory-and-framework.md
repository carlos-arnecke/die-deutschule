# The theory behind the level test

*For the teaching team. What the test is built on, where each question's level comes from, what the research says about the method, and — just as important — what this test is honestly **not**.*

Written 16 September 2026; updated 21 September 2026 for v1.2. Sources are listed at the end and linked inline.

---

## 1. The short version

- **The levels are the CEFR's.** A1–C1 mean what the Council of Europe's *Common European Framework of Reference* says they mean. The CEFR itself, though, describes what a learner *can do* — it is deliberately language-neutral and contains no list of German grammar.
- **The German grammar-per-level mapping comes from the official German implementations of the CEFR**: *Profile deutsch* (the Council of Europe's Reference Level Description for German) and the exam specifications that build on it — telc's and the Goethe-Institut's. For A1 and A2 we checked every question against telc's published grammar inventory line by line. For B1 we use the Zertifikat Deutsch tradition and *Profile deutsch*. For B2 and C1 there is no official grammar list — on purpose, and both telc and Goethe say so in writing — so those questions test what the CEFR says B2 and C1 *are*: range, precision, register, idiom.
- **The method is a multistage adaptive test**, a well-established design in educational measurement. Its two defining features — routing on the number of correct answers in a short set, and letting people go back within a set — are exactly what the literature describes.
- **Self-assessment is used to choose where the test starts, never to decide the level.** That is what the research on self-assessment supports: it is reliable enough to route, not reliable enough to place.
- **It is not an exam and it has not been formally validated.** In the Council of Europe's own terms for linking a test to the CEFR, we have done the *specification* stage — mapping content to the levels — and none of the later stages. The result is an informed estimate, and the copy says so at both ends.

## 2. The framework: CEFR, and what it does and doesn't give us

### 2.1 What the CEFR is

The *Common European Framework of Reference for Languages* (Council of Europe, 2001; expanded in the *Companion Volume*, 2020) defines six levels, A1 to C2, through "can-do" descriptors — statements about what a learner can do with the language in real situations. It is **action-oriented**: it describes competence, not grammar. The Companion Volume added descriptors for mediation and online interaction and refined the "plus" levels (A2+, B1+, B2+), but did not change the six-level structure.

Two things follow that matter for a test like ours:

1. **The CEFR does not tell you which German structures belong at which level.** It says a B1 learner "can deal with most situations likely to arise whilst travelling"; it does not say a B1 learner knows the Konjunktiv II. That mapping has to come from somewhere else.
2. **A grammar test can only ever be a proxy for a CEFR level.** The level is defined by what people can *do*; grammar, vocabulary and reading are correlates of that. This is why the test cannot claim to *be* a CEFR assessment — only to estimate one.

### 2.2 Where the German grammar mapping comes from: Reference Level Descriptions

The Council of Europe commissioned language-specific implementations of the CEFR, called **Reference Level Descriptions (RLDs)**. They "identify the forms of a given language (words, grammar and so on), mastery of which corresponds to the competences defined by the CEFR" — level by level ([Council of Europe, RLDs](https://www.coe.int/en/web/common-european-framework-reference-languages/reference-level-descriptions)).

For German the RLD is ***Profile deutsch*** (Glaboniat, Müller, Rusch, Schmitz & Wertenschlag; Langenscheidt, version 2.0, 2005). It contains the can-do statements, the vocabulary and the grammar for A1–C2, each assigned to a level, on a searchable CD-ROM. It is the document the German exam providers cite as their basis:

- The **Goethe-Institut** describes its C1 alignment as a comparison of exam objectives "mit den Kannbeschreibungen für C1 in Profile deutsch … die sich auf den Referenzrahmen des Europarats stützen", supplemented by external expert review (Goethe-Zertifikat C1, *Prüfungsziele Testbeschreibung*, §3.1).
- **telc** cites *Profile deutsch* alongside the CEFR, *Waystage/Threshold* and the *Zertifikat Deutsch* as the foundations of its A1/A2 objectives (telc *Start Deutsch 1·2 Prüfungsziele Testbeschreibung*, bibliography).

So the chain of authority is: **CEFR → Profile deutsch → telc / Goethe exam specifications → this test.** We did not invent the levels; we inherited them from the people whose exams our students go on to sit.

### 2.3 The A1/A2 inventory we checked against

telc publishes a full grammar inventory for A1 and A2 in *Start Deutsch 1·2 Prüfungsziele Testbeschreibung*, chapter 6 ("Inventare — Grammatik"). Entries without an asterisk are A1; entries marked * are additionally required at A2. The relevant lines, verbatim from that chapter:

| Structure | telc level |
|---|---|
| Präsens aller Verben; Modalverben Präsens (können, wollen, müssen, dürfen, sollen, möchten) | A1 |
| Perfekt of a short list (arbeiten, bleiben, essen, fahren, fragen, glauben, haben, lesen, lernen, machen, schlafen, **sehen**, passieren, trinken, verstehen) | A1 |
| Perfekt aller Verben | A2* |
| Präteritum: haben/sein (1st/3rd person) | A1 |
| Präteritum: kommen, sagen, Modalverben | A2* |
| Konjunktiv II — as fixed lexical chunks only (hätte gern, könnten Sie, ich möchte, ich würde gern), "noch nicht systematisch" | A1/A2 |
| Passiv (receptive: "Das Auto wird repariert") | A2* |
| Verben mit trennbarem Präfix ("Kommst du mit?", "Wann fängt der Kurs an?") | A1 |
| Kasus: Nominativ, Akkusativ | A1 |
| Kasus: Dativ | A2* |
| Genitiv: proper names only ("Karls Freunde"); otherwise receptive | A1 / A2* |
| Adjektiv attributiv (endings after definite/indefinite article) | A2* |
| Komparation | A2* |
| Reflexivpronomen | A2* |
| Verbzweitstellung, Satzklammer, Negation, Fragesatz | A1 |
| Satzverbindungen: und, oder, aber, denn, dann | A1 |
| Satzverbindungen: deshalb, weil, wenn | A2* |

One sentence from that chapter is worth every teacher reading, because it describes our test's limits exactly: the inventory "bezieht sich … in erster Linie auf die Aufgabenstellung im Bereich der rezeptiven Fertigkeiten"; for speaking and writing "ist die Grammatik-Liste dagegen von untergeordneter Bedeutung." A recognition test — which is what ours is — measures receptive control of these forms, and receptive control runs ahead of productive control at every level.

### 2.4 Why there is no inventory for B2 and C1

Both major providers say, in writing, that they deliberately do not list grammar above B1:

> "Auf den Versuch einer Erweiterung der Inventare von Wortschatz und Grammatik durch ergänzende Listen wurde bewusst verzichtet, da auf diesem Niveau … jegliche Festlegung über das für B1 definierte Niveau hinaus gleichermaßen beliebig wie unvollständig wäre."
> — telc, *Handbuch telc Deutsch B2* (2019), §4

> "Wortschatz- und Grammatikinventare zum Goethe-Zertifikat C1 gibt es aus folgenden Gründen nicht: Auf dieser Stufe läßt sich keine verbindliche Eingrenzung des Wortschatzes vornehmen, da authentische Texte verwendet werden."
> — Goethe-Institut, *Goethe-Zertifikat C1 Prüfungsziele Testbeschreibung*, §4.4

What *is* specified at those levels is the quality of language use. The CEFR's "grammatical accuracy" descriptor for B2 — quoted in the telc B2 handbook — is "gute Beherrschung der Grammatik; gelegentliche Ausrutscher oder nichtsystematische Fehler". For C1, Goethe lists among the defining descriptors: "Kann ein breites Spektrum idiomatischer Wendungen und umgangssprachlicher Ausdrucksformen verstehen und Registerwechsel richtig beurteilen", and characterises C1 writing as "eine größere Bandbreite syntaktischer Strukturen" and "ein differenzierterer Umgang mit mehr oder weniger formellen Registern".

That is why our B2 and C1 questions look different from the A1–B1 ones. They are not "harder grammar rules"; they test **range** (Partizipialattribute, modal passive, genitive relative pronouns), **precision** (steigen/steigern/erhöhen; zielführend/zielstrebig), **register** (Konjunktiv I in reported speech, Nominalstil, conditional inversion), **idiom** (auf taube Ohren stoßen, in Kauf nehmen) and **stance in reading**. Each is anchored to one of those descriptors rather than to a list.

## 3. Question-by-question: where each level comes from

The full bank is printed in `for-teachers-review.md`. Every question there now carries a **"Basis"** line naming the framework reference it was checked against. The summary:

### A1 — all ten check against the telc A1 inventory
sein-conjugation, article gender, verb conjugation (Präsens), W-questions, haben, accusative indefinite article, Verbzweitstellung, everyday vocabulary from the A1 word list, sein vs. haben, local preposition *in*. All are un-starred (A1) entries.

### A2 — checked against the telc A2* entries; three questions changed in v1.1
Seven were clean: Dativ after *mit*, Dativ object after *helfen*, *weil* with verb-final order, Komparativ, *sich interessieren für* (reflexive + fixed preposition), *wenn*-clause with inversion, Perfekt participle of *kommen*.

Three were at the A1/A2 boundary and have been replaced:

| Old | Problem per telc inventory | New (v1.1) |
|---|---|---|
| A2-01 *habe … gesehen* | *sehen* is on the **A1** Perfekt list | *habe … verloren* — Perfekt of a verb not on the A1 list |
| A2-06 *Der Zug fährt … ab* | separable verbs are **A1** | *Wir sind nach Hamburg gefahren* — Perfekt with *sein* (Perfekt aller Verben, A2*) |
| A2-10 *muss ich noch arbeiten* | all six modal verbs in the Präsens are **A1** | *Früher wollte ich …* — Präteritum of a modal verb (A2*) |

### B1 — checked against Profile deutsch / Zertifikat Deutsch; two flagged for you
Irrealis with *wenn* + Konjunktiv II (systematic KII, beyond the A2 chunks), relative clause in the accusative, genitive preposition *trotz*, *damit* purpose clause, *sich freuen auf*, inversion after a fronted *obwohl*-clause, *ausfüllen*, and a gist-reading item written to the B1 descriptor "kann … Texte verstehen, in denen vor allem sehr gebräuchliche Alltags- oder Berufssprache vorkommt".

Two are at the **A2/B1 boundary** and we want your judgement rather than ours:

- **B1-03, Passiv Präsens** — telc lists the passive as A2* *receptively*. *Netzwerk neu* and the Zertifikat Deutsch tradition teach it at B1. Our question asks the learner to *select* the auxiliary, which is closer to productive knowledge. We have kept it at B1.
- **B1-04, adjective endings** — telc lists attributive adjective endings as A2*. In practice they are consolidated through B1 and every B1 exam scores them. Kept at B1; tell us if you would move it.

### B2 and C1 — anchored to descriptors, not a list
See §2.4. Each question's "Basis" line names the descriptor: range of syntax, accuracy, precision of vocabulary, register, idiom, or reading for implicit stance. The two reading passages were written to the B2 descriptor ("kann … Artikel und Berichte über aktuelle Fragen lesen, in denen bestimmte Haltungen oder Standpunkte vertreten werden") and the C1 descriptor ("feinere Nuancen auch von explizit oder implizit angesprochenen Einstellungen und Meinungen erfassen").

## 4. The method: a multistage adaptive test

### 4.1 What the design is called

The test is a **multistage test (MST)** with number-correct routing. Hendrickson's NCME instructional module (2007) describes the family: sets of items "administered adaptively and … scored as a unit"; a short routing stage estimates ability and sends the examinee to a next stage "with difficulty values that best match this ability". MST has "all of the advantages of adaptive testing … without many of the disadvantages of an item-level adaptive test". The standard reference work is Yan, von Davier & Lewis (eds.), *Computerized Multistage Testing: Theory and Applications* (2014).

Our version:

- **Stages** = the five level-sets (A1 … C1), six questions each.
- **Routing rule** = number correct: ≥5 climb, 3–4 stop, ≤2 descend.
- **Entry stage** chosen by self-assessment (see §5), not by a routing test — a simplification that DIALANG also makes.

### 4.2 Two things the literature specifically endorses that we do

**Reviewing answers within a stage.** The MST literature names this as an advantage over item-level adaptive tests: because a set is scored as a unit, examinees can "review and change answers within a stage". That is our Back button — and it is also why Back does *not* cross a set boundary. Once a set has routed you, changing its answers would let you walk the test upward by trial and error.

**Recovery from a bad start.** A staged design can be built so that an initial misrouting is corrected in later stages. Ours descends as well as climbs, and we verified by simulation that every combination of starting set and true ability arrives at the same level. The self-assessment changes how long the test takes, never the result.

### 4.3 Where we are thinner than the literature would like

Operational MSTs typically use **more items per stage** than six. Six is a deliberate trade against completion rate — this is a lead magnet on a phone, and every extra question loses people — but it means each routing decision rests on little evidence. The 5-of-6 threshold to climb is strict partly to compensate. If we ever see that people are being placed too high, the first lever is eight items per set, not a different design.

## 5. Self-assessment: what it's good for

### 5.1 The evidence

Ross (1998) meta-analysed 60 correlations between learners' self-assessment and their test performance and found an average correlation of about 0.6 — meaningful, but with wide variation between studies, and consistently **higher for receptive skills than for speaking and writing**. Li & Zhang (2021), with a larger set of studies, found a moderate overall correlation of roughly 0.5, again varying with how the self-assessment was designed.

The practical reading: self-assessment predicts level well enough to be *useful* and not well enough to be *decisive*.

### 5.2 How the test uses it — and doesn't

**Routing only.** The "how much German do you already know?" question decides which set you see first. Nothing else. A wrong self-estimate costs time, not accuracy (§4.2).

This is the same division of labour DIALANG makes. DIALANG — the Council of Europe–aligned diagnostic system developed by twenty European universities (Alderson, 2005) — runs a vocabulary-size placement test and CEFR self-assessment statements *before* the skill test, and uses them to decide which level of test to administer, not to report a result.

That is the only place self-assessment enters the test. An earlier design added three can-do statements after the test (speaking, writing, listening) to flag a gap between grammar and speaking confidence; they were dropped because they never changed the level, only added a nudge towards the trial lesson that the result already gives every learner from A2 up. Three screens of friction for a marginal nudge is a bad trade on a first contact.

## 6. Question format: three options or four?

The best evidence favours **three**. Rodriguez (2005) meta-analysed 80 years of research and concluded that "three options are optimal for multiple-choice items": additional distractors are rarely functional, and the time they cost is better spent on more items. telc's own grammar section (*Sprachbausteine*) uses three-option multiple choice.

We use **four**, for a reason specific to this design, and it comes with a condition:

- With only six items per set and a 5-of-6 bar to climb, a learner who can eliminate one option and guess between the rest clears the bar about **11%** of the time with three options and under **2%** with four. In a long test that difference washes out; in a six-item routing set it is the difference between a placement and a coin toss.
- **The condition:** a fourth option only does this work if it is a genuine distractor. A filler fourth looks like four options and functions as three. So when you review the bank, the fourth option is the one to be hardest on — and if you find many of them weak, the right response is to drop back to three, not to keep a fake fourth.

## 7. What "aligned to the CEFR" would formally require — and where we stand

The Council of Europe's *Manual for Relating Language Examinations to the CEFR* (2009) sets out four stages:

| Stage | What it means | This test |
|---|---|---|
| **Familiarisation** | The people building the test know the CEFR levels well | Yes — this document, and you |
| **Specification** | The test's content is mapped to the levels, with evidence | Yes — §2–3 above; every item carries its basis |
| **Standardisation** | Trained judges rate real performances / items against calibrated CEFR samples; cut-scores are set by a formal procedure | **No** |
| **Validation** | Empirical evidence that the test's results agree with independent CEFR-level judgements | **No** |

We are honest about this in the product: the copy says "estimate" and "not an official placement" at the start and the end, and never "CEFR-certified". The gap is closable — see §9 — but it has not been closed.

## 8. What the test does not do, and why the design is the way it is

- **It measures recognition, not production.** A multiple-choice item shows that a learner can *identify* the right form. Producing it in speech is a separate, harder skill; the telc inventory quoted in §2.3 says as much. Hence the disclaimers, the trial lesson, and the teacher's confirmation on day one.
- **It does not measure speaking, listening or writing at all.** Which is why the result sends everyone from A2 up to a free trial lesson, and why the teacher confirms the level on day one.
- **The alternative design we considered — a C-test — is the one the German placement research actually favours.** onSET (formerly onDaF), the placement test used by German university language centres, is a C-test: gapped texts where the learner completes half-words. Eckes & Grotjahn's validation shows C-tests fit a Rasch scale and correlate 0.5–0.7 with all four skills. We did not use it in v1 because it needs typed input, which is slow and error-prone on a phone and unfriendly to a lead magnet. It remains the strongest candidate for an in-school placement module later.

---

## 9. What would make it better — in order

1. **Your review of the bank** (in `for-teachers-review.md`), with particular attention to the fourth options and to the two boundary items in §3.
2. **Collect the data.** The test already records every answer. Once about a hundred real people have taken it, we can see which items behave — an item that B1-placed students get right at the same rate as A2-placed students is doing nothing and should be replaced. That is the beginning of *validation*.
3. **Compare against you.** For every student who takes the test and then starts a course, record the level the teacher confirms on day one. Agreement between the two is the single number that tells us whether the test works. This is cheap and it is the validation stage in §7.
4. **Eight items per set** if step 3 shows over-placement.
5. **A C-test module** for in-school use, where typing is fine and a more precise instrument is worth ten more minutes.
